2026-09-16 surface-view ground normal maps:
moon-lola-normal.jpg and mars-mola-normal.jpg are not new source imagery -
each is a tangent-space normal map computed offline from the mission
elevation data already listed above (tools/build-surface-normals.mjs; the
gradient math is in src/surface-normal-detail.js). They replace Three.js's
screen-space bump approximation, which goes flat once a texel of a
several-thousand-kilometre map covers less than a screen pixel - exactly
the situation standing on the ground puts it in. Earth and every other body
without a shipped elevation map get the equivalent normal map computed from
their existing procedural relief field instead of new imagery.

Every body's ground also gets a second, much finer normal layer blended in
close up (src/surface-detail.js, applyMicroDetail): generic rock/regolith
grain, not modelled on any specific body, generated once in-code
(seamlessHeightField in src/surface-normal-detail.js) and tiled hundreds of
times across the surface. It is a cosmetic detail layer so the ground right
under a standing observer - always far inside a single texel of even the
real elevation maps above - doesn't read as a smooth interpolation of its
far-away neighbours. No third-party asset.

2026-09-16 known-places navigation and sky-object labelling:
The "Known places" list in the object details pane (src/surface-places.js)
jumps a surface-view visit straight to a real, named surface feature instead
of latitude/longitude zero. Names and approximate planetocentric
coordinates follow the IAU's official planetary nomenclature,
https://planetarynames.wr.usgs.gov/ - rounded to the nearest degree, which
is enough to bring a feature into view from directly overhead but is not
survey-grade placement. A few (Jupiter's Great Red Spot, Neptune's Great
Dark Spot, Saturn's polar hexagon, Io's active volcanoes) name features
that drift or evolve, so their coordinates are only approximate for the
current epoch. Proteus and Nereid, only glimpsed by Voyager 2, have no
confidently mapped named feature; each instead points at the sub-planetary
point, since this simulation already models every moon without its own
tabulated rotation as tidally locked.

Standing in surface view, the same solar-system bodies the radar already
tracks, and - opt-in, matching the existing deep-sky marker toggle - deep-sky
objects, now also get a name-and-outline marker drawn directly over their
actual position in the rendered sky (src/sky-labels.js), rather than only on
the radar or in the object list. No new imagery or data; it projects
positions the simulation already computes.

2026-09-16 sphere-based surface-view radar:
The flat 2D compass ring (bearing only) is replaced by a small rendered
sky-sphere (src/surface-radar.js) that plots each tracked body's full 3D
direction - bearing and altitude/pitch together - as a dot on a dome

imagery or data; it is a second small Three.js scene, following the same
reusable-renderer pattern as the existing detail preview, projecting
positions the simulation already computes.

2026-09-16 a denser star field and a wider deep-sky catalogue:

source catalogue itself stops.

the catalogue is. Every addition gets its own researched note in all four
interface languages (src/sky-descriptions.js), the same as the original 32.

2026-09-16 more detail in the Milky Way band:
sky/milkyway.bin's point cloud grows from 110 000 to 500 000 points
(tools/build-sky.mjs, buildMilkyWay), restoring more of the grain of the
individual stars that resolve out of the band - there is far more of that

2026-09-16 deep-sky labels keep up with a dragged camera:
The name-and-outline overlay for deep-sky objects (above) is a DOM layer,
not part of the WebGL scene, so it was only repainted every 6th frame while
the scene itself redraws every frame regardless - a deliberate saving, since
the scene changes anyway and the overlay usually does not. But that made no
exception for a dragged camera: the 3D view tracked the drag every frame
while the labels visibly lagged behind it by up to those 6 frames. main.js
now also repaints immediately whenever the camera has actually rotated since
the last paint, detected with a new pure helper, quaternionChanged (in
src/sky-labels.js, kept framework-free like the rest of that module): the
angle between the previous and current camera orientation, from their
quaternions' dot product, checked against a small epsilon so floating-point
noise alone never triggers a repaint. The periodic every-6th-frame repaint
still runs as a fallback for changes the camera orientation does not
capture, such as the horizon filter drifting while standing on a surface as
simulated time passes.

2026-09-16 make Saturn and Uranus's rings match their real structure:
Saturn's ring previously used approximate hand-picked band boundaries, and
Uranus's used a single continuous translucent disc coloured by a repeating
sine wave - neither sourced from real ring geometry. Both are rewritten in
a new module, planet-rings.js (with its own tests), from Cassini/Voyager
and stellar-occultation radii (Wikipedia, "Rings of Saturn" and "Rings of
Uranus"), in units of each planet's own equatorial radius (Saturn 60,300
km, Uranus 25,559 km): the D, C, B, Cassini Division, A ring, Encke Gap,
Keeler Gap, Roche Division and F ring for Saturn; the zeta, 6, 5, 4, alpha,
beta, eta, gamma, delta, lambda and epsilon rings for Uranus. Each band's
alpha now stands in for its measured normal optical depth (roughly
alpha=1-e^-tau) instead of a single flat opacity for the whole ring, using
a 4-component (RGBA) vertex-colour attribute - three.js applies per-vertex
alpha automatically once a "color" attribute has 4 components instead of
3, so Saturn's Cassini/Encke/Keeler gaps and the wide true emptiness
between every named Uranus ring are now actually transparent, not just a
darker but still-solid tone painted over the same disc.
This also fixes a real, previously-alarming inaccuracy: Uranus's outer
ring boundary was 1.9 planet radii, which entirely excluded the epsilon
ring at ~2.00 radii - Uranus's brightest, widest and most famous ring, the
one whose 1977 stellar-occultation detection led to discovering the whole
system. It is now included, along with the true, very wide gaps between
Uranus's other narrow rings (most are only a few kilometres wide in
reality and are drawn here as a deliberately widened band at their correct
position for visibility, same as Saturn's Encke and Keeler gaps already
were - but the emptiness between them is real, not a rendering shortcut).

2026-09-16 let a planet cast its own shadow onto its own rings:
Saturn and Uranus's rings already carried a ring-specific eclipse shader
(ringEclipseShadow, alongside the same extended-solar-shadow system used
for planet-on-planet eclipses), but updateExtendedSolarShadows() built one
candidate list per body and fed it to both that body's own eclipse shader
and its ring's, using the body's own id as the "receiver" for both.
solarOccludersForReceiver() excludes any occluder whose id matches the
receiver - correct for a body's own mesh, which cannot shadow itself - but
it also silently excluded the planet from ever appearing as a candidate
occluder for its own ring, so the dark band a planet casts across part of
its rings when it blocks the Sun - one of the most recognisable eclipse
effects in the solar system - never rendered, no matter how the real
Sun-planet-ring geometry lined up.
The ring's candidate list is now built with a receiver id no real body
ever has, so the planet is no longer excluded from shadowing its own
rings, while the planet's own mesh keeps excluding itself exactly as
before. Confirmed by inspecting the shader's own occluder-count uniform
before and after: previously 0 candidates for a planet's own ring, now 1
(the planet itself, at the expected radius) for both Saturn and Uranus.

2026-09-16 real solar eclipse scenarios ("Zaćmienia"):
The occlusion, penumbra/umbra and shadow-sweep needed no new physics at
all: a solar eclipse is just the Moon, a real 3D body at its real
ELP-2000/82 position (lunar-theory.js), passing in front of the Sun as
seen from a point on Earth's own real 3D surface, and the same per-fragment
eclipse shader already used for a planet's ring shadow (
extended-solar-shadow.js) already treats Earth as an ordinary receiver.
What was missing was a way to land on a date where that geometry is worth
watching. The new "Zaćmienia" scenario picker (src/solar-eclipses.js) lists
six real eclipses - three total, one annular and one hybrid, from 2017 to
2027 - each with the kind, the UTC instant of greatest eclipse, the
duration of totality/annularity there and the geographic point of greatest
eclipse, as tabulated in NASA's Eclipse Website (F. Espenak's catalogue)
and mirrored on each event's Wikipedia page. Launching one rewinds the
whole system to a head start before that instant (a practical viewing
margin, not itself a sourced contact time), stands the viewer at the real
point of greatest eclipse in a real-scale surface view, and aims the
camera at the Sun.
Two things were not obvious in advance. First, beginEarthSurfaceContext()
unconditionally resets the clock to the device's actual "now" the moment
Earth's surface view opens - reasonable for a viewer who just wants
tonight's real sky, wrong for a scripted date - so the chosen instant has
to be reapplied immediately afterwards, once surface view has finished its
own setup. Second, at the Sun's true half-degree apparent size the surface
view's default field of view draws it a few pixels across: correct, but too
small to see the Moon actually cross it, and standing still would let the
sky's rotation carry both out of frame well before totality. The scenario
therefore opens already zoomed to the surface view's closest allowed field
of view, and re-aims at the brightest object above the horizon every few
frames for as long as the scenario runs (surfaceView.trackBrightest) - the
only surface-view case that ever moves the camera on its own; every other
surface view keeps the exact fixed, hand-aimed direction it always did.
Checked against the least ambiguous test available - a known real eclipse
should show the Sun and Moon converge to the same point in the sky at the
real, published instant of greatest eclipse, and separate again on either
side of it - by reading each body's projected screen position
(window.solare.getView()) through a run of the 2024-04-08 event: the two
discs were 20 px apart 50 minutes before greatest eclipse, arrived within
half a pixel of each other within a minute of the published 18:18:29 UTC,
and were separating again shortly after.
Sources: NASA Eclipse Website / F. Espenak's five-millennium catalogue,
cross-checked against Wikipedia's infobox for each listed eclipse
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse_of_August_21,_2017 and the
equivalent pages for 2023-04-20, 2023-10-14, 2024-04-08, 2026-08-12 and
2027-08-02).

2026-09-16 real lunar eclipse scenarios:
The same "Zaćmienia" picker gained a second, lunar half (src/lunar-eclipses.js),
listing seven real eclipses from 2022 to 2028 - four total, one partial and
one penumbral - each with its kind and the sourced instant of greatest
eclipse plus the umbral/partial/penumbral durations there, again from NASA's
Eclipse Website and cross-checked on Wikipedia. A lunar eclipse needed no
surface view: it is visible from an entire hemisphere at once rather than one
narrow path, so launching one rewinds the system to a head start before
greatest eclipse (half the sourced penumbral duration, since the Moon
crosses the shadow at close to constant speed near mid-eclipse) and simply
follows the Moon from outside - in real scale, forced on for the same reason
surface view always forces it: compressed mode distorts exactly the relative
sizes and distances the eclipse shader's overlap calculation depends on.
Earth's shadow on the Moon needed no new code at all, for the same reason a
planet's shadow on its own rings did not: updateExtendedSolarShadows()
already builds a full occluder candidate list for every receiving body, and
the Moon already receives that shadow (participatesInSolarShadow is true for
every body but the Sun and a black hole) with Earth as an ordinary, un-excluded
candidate. This is also why the very same self-shadow bug fixed for a
planet's rings a moment earlier plainly never affected the Moon: the excluded
receiver id there was always the ring's own placeholder, never the real
Earth-Moon pair.
Checked the same way as the solar scenario: reading the raw body positions
(window.solare.getState()) through a run of the 2022-11-08 event, the
Earth-Moon-Sun angle (how far Earth sits from directly blocking the Sun, as
seen from the Moon) fell from 1.45 degrees at the scenario's start to 0.96
degrees roughly an hour later - closing steadily toward zero on schedule for
the published 10:59:08 UTC greatest eclipse - and a screenshot partway
through already shows a real, sourced-geometry shadow visibly advancing
across the Moon's disc.
Sources: NASA Eclipse Website / F. Espenak's five-millennium catalogue,
cross-checked against Wikipedia's infobox for each listed eclipse
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/November_2022_lunar_eclipse and the
equivalent pages for 2025-03-14, 2025-09-07, 2026-03-03, 2026-08-28,
2027-02-20 and 2028-12-31).

2026-09-16 planets and moons keeping their level of detail current while the
camera moves:
Every body's sphere mesh is swapped between a low/medium/high vertex count
(scene-lod.js) depending on how large it is currently drawn, so a distant
moon a couple of pixels across never pays for a 56x40 sphere and a planet
filling the screen never shows a visibly faceted one. That check itself was
only run for a given body on one frame in every four (frame&3 matched
against the body's own id), the same kind of periodic throttle that once
let the deep-sky name labels visibly lag behind a dragged camera - and for
the same reason: the camera can move every single frame (a held scroll
wheel, a fast drag), but a body's rendered detail only followed up to three
frames later, long enough to notice as the planet briefly looking blockier
or smoother than it should for the distance the camera actually reached.
The fix is simpler than the deep-sky one, because there is no cheap way here
to detect "changed enough" other than doing the real distance check in the
first place - and that check (one distanceTo, one tan, one comparison) is
negligible next to the rest of a frame's work even at this app's full body
count, so it is no longer throttled at all: every body's projected size,
and with it its level of detail, is re-evaluated on every single frame.
Checked by exposing each body's current level (window.solare().getView().
bodies[].lod) and driving a fast, continuous zoom (repeated wheel events)
toward the inner planets for 90 consecutive animation frames while sampling
every frame: with the throttle in place this produced 25 frames where a

2026-09-25 Share works again:
Share did nothing because main.js declares its own function location(e)
(the click-point raycast), which shadows window.location inside the module:
copySharedSimulation passed that function to new URL() and threw before
copying anything, and the same shadowing meant an opened shared link was
never read. Both calls now name window.location. The prompt() fallback for
a refused clipboard (refused, like prompt itself, in embedded viewers and on
plain-http LAN addresses) is replaced by a small in-page dialog that shows
the link selected, with its own Copy button and the older copy command as a
second attempt.
Verified with node --test and a Playwright run: the copied link restores
the shared state, and with the clipboard refused the dialog opens with the
link selected.

2026-09-25 Moons orbit the right way round, in their planets' equators:
Every regular moon of Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and
Neptune was launched with its velocity pointing the wrong way, so all of
them went round clockwise seen from ecliptic north (175 degrees), in one
plane per planet tipped about a different axis from the planet's own spin
axis - Saturn's moons crossed its rings at 27 degrees, Uranus's at 90, and
Triton, the one real retrograde moon, came out prograde. src/planet-poles.js
now gives each planet a spin axis with its tabulated tilt in the direction
of its IAU pole (the rotational elements surface-frame.js already had),
and each moon is placed on a Keplerian ellipse around that axis with its
own inclination and eccentricity (MOON_ORBITS, NASA/JPL mean elements via
each moon's Wikipedia page); retrograde motion comes from the inclination
itself. Nereid is referred to the ecliptic, Iapetus to its own Laplace
plane 14.8 degrees from Saturn's equator; the Moon keeps lunar-theory.js.
Assumptions: the tabulated tilt stays the editable obliquity, so a spin
axis can sit up to its planet's orbital inclination (7 degrees for
Mercury, under 3 elsewhere) from the exact IAU pole; lines of nodes and
phases of moons without an ephemeris are composed. The renderer orients
each body's axis group from the same function, so rings, ring particles,
equators and moon orbits agree by construction; share links carry the pole
direction too. Io's speed test now allows its real ±0.4% eccentric swing.
Verified with node --test (448 passing, none failing), a year-long
collision run of all 47 bodies, npm run build, and screenshots of Saturn,
Uranus and Jupiter with orbits shown.

2026-09-25 Physics vectors on every moving body in view:
The Physics vectors toggle drew one velocity and one gravity arrow, for the
selected body only. It now draws a pair on every body inside the camera's
view frustum that moves relative to its primary - its parent for a moon,
otherwise the most massive other body, so planets are measured against the
Sun and each star of a binary against its companion; a body ten times
heavier than everything else (the Sun) is the centre and gets none.
Arrows are sized on screen (38 px, 72 px for the selected body, never
shorter than the body itself) because at system scale Saturn's moons sit a
hair apart; the selected body's pair is opaque and the rest slightly
translucent, and the physics card still describes the selected body. A pool
of arrow pairs is reused, updated every other frame. The pure parts live in
src/education-vectors.js with tests/education-vectors.test.js. In the
redesigned interface the physics card moves bottom-left, clear of the
camera controls.

2026-09-25 The Moon stays visible once its map loads:
The Moon - and every moon with a mission map - vanished, together with its
thumbnail in the details panel, as soon as its map finished loading.
applyMoonAppearance ran a second time on the same material when the map
arrived and wrapped onBeforeCompile again, so the recompiled fragment shader
declared moonBase and moonDark twice and failed to compile. The appearance is
now installed once per material (userData.moonAppearance); the loader only
assigns the map. Test: tests/moon-shader-reload.test.js.

2026-09-25 Asteroids and ring particles are no longer black:
Main-belt asteroids, Saturn's ring particles and the irregular-moon swarm
rendered as black silhouettes: their materials had vertexColors:true, which
multiplies by the geometry's colour attribute, and the icosahedron has none,
so WebGL read (0,0,0). Their tones come from instanceColor alone now.
Test: tests/instanced-rock-colours.test.js.
