2026-09-07 visible-light appearance audit:
Original illustration maps remain unchanged on disk. The white solar photosphere
is procedural, so its unused source map is not shipped. Venus,
Uranus and Neptune use documented approximate visible-light display profiles;
Mars saturation is reduced. These are not spectrally calibrated products.
Sources and limitations: COLOR-AUDIT.md in the source repository.

2026-09-08 satellite appearance audit:
14 mission mosaics in textures/moons/*.jpg: USGS Astrogeology, from NASA
Viking, Voyager, Galileo and Cassini imaging; Phobos control by DLR.
Exact download URLs and WMS layers: textures/moons/sources.json.
Monochrome or enhanced mosaics provide structure, not calibrated visible RGB.
Display approximations and two references for each included moon:
MOON-APPEARANCE.md in the source repository.
Titan displays an opaque atmospheric approximation, not a radar surface map.
Unmapped moons and exoplanets do not reuse Earth's Moon craters.

Solar interior: educational opaque radiation-field schematic, not imagery
or radiative-transfer simulation. Structure and approximate temperatures:
https://solarscience.msfc.nasa.gov/interior.shtml
https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2020/01/Anatomy_of_the_Sun

Surface-view detail maps (loaded only while standing on the body):
Earth: NASA SVS Blue Marble, 4K derivative of the 8192×4096 static mosaic,
https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4439/
Moon: NASA SVS CGI Moon Kit — LROC WAC natural-colour mosaic and LOLA DEM,
https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4720/
Mars: NASA SVS MOLA global elevation map, reduced to a single-channel 2K
relief texture for interactive rendering,
https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4436/
The Moon relief map is mission elevation data. Other bodies use a compact,
deterministic relief field only where a comparable global DEM is not shipped.

2026-09-16 surface-view ground normal maps:
moon-lola-normal.jpg and mars-mola-normal.jpg are not new source imagery -
each is a tangent-space normal map computed offline from the mission
elevation data already listed above (tools/build-surface-normals.mjs; the
gradient math is in src/surface-normal-detail.js). They replace Three.js's
screen-space bump approximation, which goes flat once a texel of a
several-thousand-kilometre map covers less than a screen pixel - exactly
the situation standing on the ground puts it in. Earth and every other body
without a shipped elevation map get the equivalent normal map computed from
their existing procedural relief field instead of new imagery.

Every body's ground also gets a second, much finer normal layer blended in
close up (src/surface-detail.js, applyMicroDetail): generic rock/regolith
grain, not modelled on any specific body, generated once in-code
(seamlessHeightField in src/surface-normal-detail.js) and tiled hundreds of
times across the surface. It is a cosmetic detail layer so the ground right
under a standing observer - always far inside a single texel of even the
real elevation maps above - doesn't read as a smooth interpolation of its
far-away neighbours. No third-party asset.

2026-09-16 known-places navigation and sky-object labelling:
The "Known places" list in the object details pane (src/surface-places.js)
jumps a surface-view visit straight to a real, named surface feature instead
of latitude/longitude zero. Names and approximate planetocentric
coordinates follow the IAU's official planetary nomenclature,
https://planetarynames.wr.usgs.gov/ - rounded to the nearest degree, which
is enough to bring a feature into view from directly overhead but is not
survey-grade placement. A few (Jupiter's Great Red Spot, Neptune's Great
Dark Spot, Saturn's polar hexagon, Io's active volcanoes) name features
that drift or evolve, so their coordinates are only approximate for the
current epoch. Proteus and Nereid, only glimpsed by Voyager 2, have no
confidently mapped named feature; each instead points at the sub-planetary
point, since this simulation already models every moon without its own
tabulated rotation as tidally locked.

Standing in surface view, the same solar-system bodies the radar already
tracks, and - opt-in, matching the existing deep-sky marker toggle - deep-sky
objects, now also get a name-and-outline marker drawn directly over their
actual position in the rendered sky (src/sky-labels.js), rather than only on
the radar or in the object list. No new imagery or data; it projects
positions the simulation already computes.

2026-09-16 sphere-based surface-view radar:
The flat 2D compass ring (bearing only) is replaced by a small rendered
sky-sphere (src/surface-radar.js) that plots each tracked body's full 3D
direction - bearing and altitude/pitch together - as a dot on a dome
standing in for the observer's surroundings, with a horizon ring and a
marker for the current look direction. The dome itself never rotates, since
a bare sphere looks the same from any heading; only the marker positions are
recomputed each repaint from the observer's current heading and the pure
projection math in src/surface-compass.js. A body below the horizon still
renders, dimmed, seen through the translucent dome, so "is it worth turning
around for" is visible at a glance rather than requiring a full turn. No new
imagery or data; it is a second small Three.js scene, following the same
reusable-renderer pattern as the existing detail preview, projecting
positions the simulation already computes.

2026-09-16 deep-sky labels shown in every view:
The name-and-outline marker for a deep-sky object (above) now follows the
same toggle's marker dots wherever they are drawn (src/sky.js) rather than
only while standing on a surface: the free orbit view, star-system presets,
the light-flight camera and falling into a black hole all get it too,
whenever "Deep-sky objects" is switched on. Standing on a surface is still
the one case that filters by altitude, since the ground genuinely blocks
anything below the horizon there; everywhere else nothing blocks the view
in any direction, so only being in front of the camera is required. No new
imagery or data; same projection as before, run every view instead of one.

2026-09-16 landmark pins on the object-details preview:
A new "Landmarks" toggle (off by default) on any body with known places
(src/surface-places.js) marks them directly on that body's small rotating
preview in the object-details panel, so the one or two most interesting
features on a planet or moon are visible at a glance, in their real place,
without opening surface view. Each pin's position comes from
src/surface-texture-frame.js's localSurfacePoint, the same equirectangular
calibration surface view already uses to orient the ground itself
(equirectangularSurfaceBasis, inverted) - so a pin lands exactly where that
calibration says the named feature's texture pixel is, and it turns with
the body's own rotation because it is added as a child of the same mesh.
No new imagery; it is a small glowing marker (src/preview.js), the same
visual language as the sky and radar markers elsewhere.

2026-09-16 a denser star field and a wider deep-sky catalogue:
sky/stars.bin is rebuilt from d3-celestial's stars.14.json instead of
stars.8.json (tools/build-sky.mjs), reaching limit magnitude 14 instead of 8
- 118 216 stars instead of 41 411. Air has extinction and light pollution;
space, where every view of this simulation's sky is set, has neither (see
the flux comment in src/sky.js, already relied on to keep faint stars
visible), so a real observer keeps resolving fainter starlight far past the
naked-eye limit rather than hitting a hard cutoff. 14 is simply where the
source catalogue itself stops.

sky/deepsky.json grows from the 32 objects in d3-celestial's own hand-picked
"bright" list to 60, adding 28 more famous entries from the rest of the
Messier catalogue (d3-celestial's messier.json, same dataset and licence) -
chosen for having an actual common name (Whirlpool Galaxy, Ring Nebula,
Crab Nebula, Sombrero Galaxy and so on) rather than for magnitude alone, and
spread across galaxies, nebulae and clusters rather than concentrated in one
type. Messier's own three unresolved star fields/asterisms (M 24, M 40,
M 73) are left out, since they are not deep-sky objects the way the rest of
the catalogue is. Every addition gets its own researched note in all four
interface languages (src/sky-descriptions.js), the same as the original 32.

2026-09-16 more detail in the Milky Way band:
sky/milkyway.bin's point cloud grows from 110 000 to 500 000 points
(tools/build-sky.mjs, buildMilkyWay), restoring more of the grain of the
individual stars that resolve out of the band - there is far more of that
grain to show away from a planet's atmosphere and light pollution than the
view from Earth's surface ever exposes. sky/milkyway.webp is rebuilt at the
working raster grid's own native resolution (2880x1440, matching the isophote
survey's own vertex spacing of roughly 0.1-0.24 degrees) instead of being
downsampled to 2048x1024 afterwards, so the shipped map keeps detail the
build already computed rather than discarding part of it. Same source data,
same isophote levels, same blur pipeline - nothing invented.

2026-09-16 deep-sky labels keep up with a dragged camera:
The name-and-outline overlay for deep-sky objects (above) is a DOM layer,
not part of the WebGL scene, so it was only repainted every 6th frame while
the scene itself redraws every frame regardless - a deliberate saving, since
the scene changes anyway and the overlay usually does not. But that made no
exception for a dragged camera: the 3D view tracked the drag every frame
while the labels visibly lagged behind it by up to those 6 frames. main.js
now also repaints immediately whenever the camera has actually rotated since
the last paint, detected with a new pure helper, quaternionChanged (in
src/sky-labels.js, kept framework-free like the rest of that module): the
angle between the previous and current camera orientation, from their
quaternions' dot product, checked against a small epsilon so floating-point
noise alone never triggers a repaint. The periodic every-6th-frame repaint
still runs as a fallback for changes the camera orientation does not
capture, such as the horizon filter drifting while standing on a surface as
simulated time passes.

2026-09-16 no more repeated description under "Photos":
A sky object's own note (its "sky-note" paragraph) and a central star's
appearance note were already shown once, higher up in the details panel -
but the same text was then also dropped into the "Photos" section's own
description line, right above the image gallery, so it read twice on the
same panel. mountWikipediaReference() in main.js takes a new
hasDescriptionElsewhere flag: when the caller already displayed this text,
the "Photos" section renders only the gallery and the Wikipedia link, never
a second copy of the description or of Wikipedia's own summary as a
fallback for it. Every other kind of body (planets, moons, asteroids,
comets, black holes) keeps its note exactly where it always was, in that
same description line, since nothing else in their panel shows it - the
fix is only for the two cases that genuinely repeated themselves.

2026-09-16 a known place overrides the device location, not the other way round:
Clicking a known place (src/surface-places.js) now aims the surface-view
camera down at the ground - 18 degrees below the horizon, so the ground
fills most of the frame but a strip of open sky stays visible above it -
instead of hunting the sky for something to sight, the way opening surface
view normally does. On Earth, that click (and dragging the latitude/
longitude sliders by hand) also marks the position as chosen: the ongoing
navigator.geolocation.watchPosition fix keeps running underneath, but a
device location that arrives afterwards can no longer silently drag a
hand-picked spot back to wherever the browser thinks the device is. The HUD
label says which is which - "Chosen location" for a manual position,
"Device location" only when it is actually the current GPS/network fix.

2026-09-16 landmark pins in the main scene are hoverable and clickable:
The pins added to a tracked body's real mesh (above) sat there but did
nothing: no name on hover, no cursor change, no click target - unlike every
other marker in the scene (a body, a deep-sky object, a constellation
figure), which all already show a tooltip and jump somewhere on click. Each
pin sprite now carries its source place in userData (preview.js), and
main.js's existing pointer-picking chain gains a landmark check ahead of
the others (a real 3D raycast against the pin group, the same technique
already used for a body, rather than sky.js's screen-space picking for a
deep-sky marker or constellation figure, since a pin already lives in
ordinary scene space right on the mesh). Hovering shows the same floating
name tag as everything else; clicking calls the same goToKnownPlace already
wired to the surface-view HUD's own known-places list, landing standing at
that exact spot and looking at the ground - answering "what is this" by
showing it, not by adding another line of text to read.
